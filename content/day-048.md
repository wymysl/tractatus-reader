## Explanation

"Aus dem Bestehen oder Nichtbestehen eines Sachverhaltes kann nicht auf
das Bestehen oder Nichtbestehen eines anderen geschlossen werden." From
the existence or non-existence of an atomic fact we cannot infer the
existence or non-existence of another.

Yesterday's thesis already announced this one. 2.061 said atomic facts
are independent of one another, then added — reaching one sentence
ahead of itself — that this is exactly what 2.062 states next, "today's
sentence supplies the reason, tomorrow's states the consequence." So
2.062 is not a fresh discovery; it is 2.061 read as a rule about
inference instead of a rule about existence. If no atomic fact's
obtaining has any bearing on another's, then no argument can run from
one to the other either — *geschlossen*, "inferred" or, more literally,
"concluded," the ordinary word for what a *Schluss*, a piece of
reasoning, does.

The consequence is worth its own day because it closes a door 2.061
left standing open. 2.061 was careful to say its independence is
logical, not causal — not a claim that atomic facts have no physical
influence on each other, a question for natural science. That carefulness
could be misread: perhaps logical independence rules out *certainty*
about one atomic fact given another, while leaving room for the weaker
thing — a correlation, a probability, something short of proof but still
an inference of a sort, grounded in how the world happens to run.
2.062 forecloses that reading too. It says flatly that no inference —
not *kann nicht sicher geschlossen werden*, "cannot be inferred with
certainty," but *kann nicht geschlossen werden*, cannot be inferred,
full stop — reaches from one atomic fact to another. What natural
science may go on to find about how facts run together is a separate
matter entirely, outside logic's business either way; 2.062 only closes
off the specific claim that logic itself could license such a
conclusion.

The thesis reaches one step past atomic facts, too, though not further
than a bare pointer belongs here. Much later, at 5.135, the identical
claim returns transposed one level up — no inference from the existence
of one *situation* to the existence of another, entirely different one
— and later still, at 6.36 and 6.37, the book states outright that
there is no necessity beyond logical necessity, that belief in a causal
nexus binding one event to the next is superstition. 2.062 is the
smallest, earliest form of that claim, stated about the world's
simplest furniture before there is a picture theory, a causality
remark, or an argument against superstition to hang it on. None of that
belongs to today's explanation; the claim earns its full weight only
where the book actually uses it.

The fork day 9 opened at 2.01 surfaces a third time and needs no fresh
airing: is "no inference is licensed" a further fact logical atomism
has found out about the world's basic items, or a rule for how
"atomic fact" and "Sachverhalt" are to be used, true simply because of
what those words have been built to mean since thesis 2? Both readings
say the same six words here; the fork was stated in full at 2.01 and
again at 2.061, and repeating the case for a third day running would
pad rather than clarify.

## Zen

Hume, in the *Treatise* and again in the first *Enquiry*: there is no
necessary connexion to be found between distinct existences. From the
fact that one event has occurred, reason alone can never yield that
another must follow or must have preceded it; whatever constant
conjunction experience does hand us is a habit of expectation, not a
demonstration. The shape of the claim matches 2.062 almost exactly —
no inference from one existent to another — and the resemblance is not
coincidence. Wittgenstein read Hume's problem through Russell, and
6.36–6.37's "belief in the causal nexus is superstition" is close
enough to Humean vocabulary that commentators regularly read the whole
stretch as a Tractarian answer to Hume's question. That connection
belongs to those later theses, not to a note on 2.062, but it is why
the resonance here is safe to call closer than an analogy.

The disanalogy is where the two projects come apart, and it matters
for what each is trying to secure. Hume's "cannot" is an epistemic
limit: reason, examined honestly, turns out not to supply the
connection we feel entitled to expect, and Hume stays agnostic on
whether some hidden necessity binds cause to effect regardless — his
target is our warrant for believing it, not the metaphysics itself.
2.062's "cannot" claims no such limit and no such agnosticism. It is a
logical fact about *Sachverhalte*, following directly from 2.061's
independence, true a priori and without appeal to what experience does
or does not deliver; the Tractatus is not reporting that inference
turns out to be unavailable on inspection, it is stating what "atomic
fact" already rules out by the kind of item it is. One tradition finds
the impossibility at the end of an empirical investigation into the
mind's actual powers; the other finds it built into the logical grammar
from the start. Both leave the door open for natural science to find
regularities anyway — Hume's habit-formed expectation, Wittgenstein's
separate business of natural law — which is exactly why neither
philosopher needs to deny that the sun will rise; only that anything
forces it to.
