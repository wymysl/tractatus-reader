## Explanation

"Aus dem Bestehen oder Nichtbestehen eines Sachverhaltes kann nicht auf
das Bestehen oder Nichtbestehen eines anderen geschlossen werden." From
the existence or non-existence of an atomic fact we cannot infer the
existence or non-existence of another.

Yesterday's sentence was a promissory note. 2.061 said atomic facts are
independent of one another, and its explanation flagged, without yet
using it, the difference between two things "independent" could mean
here: a claim about the world's furniture, or a claim about what can be
inferred from what. 2.062 cashes the note. It does not add a new fact
about atomic facts; it draws out, in so many words, what 2.061's
independence already implied for reasoning. If one atomic fact's
obtaining had any bearing on another's — if it made the other more or
less likely, let alone certain — then from the first you could infer
something about the second. 2.061 says there is no such bearing. 2.062
just says what follows: no such inference is licensed, in either
direction, from either the obtaining or the failing of one Sachverhalt
to the obtaining or failing of any other.

The two sentences are not, for all that, one thesis said twice. 2.061 is
about the atomic facts themselves — how the world's simplest layer is
arranged, one item to the next. 2.062 is about *Schluss*, inference: what
a thinker is and is not entitled to conclude, given only that one
Sachverhalt obtains. The move from the first kind of claim to the second
is a small step, but the Tractatus takes it deliberately and separately —
this is a book that will spend chapter 5 distinguishing tautology,
contradiction, and everything a proposition can say from what merely
follows from what, and 2.062 is the first place that distinction between
*what is the case* and *what can be inferred* gets its own sentence.
Confusing the two would already misread 2.061 itself: to take
independence as somehow forbidding correct guesses would be to hear a
claim about the world as if it silently included a claim about minds and
their reasoning. 2.062 keeps them apart by stating the second explicitly,
rather than leaving it to be assumed.

Worth being precise about what is and is not being denied. Nothing here
says a thinker cannot *believe*, guess, or bet on one atomic fact given
another — only that no such belief is licensed by inference from the
bare logical relations among atomic facts themselves, because on 2.061
there are none to license it. Nor is this a claim about *events* in time,
or about cause and effect in the sciences; *Sachverhalt* is not yet
*Ereignis*, and this thesis is not the Tractatus's verdict on natural
causality — that discussion is chapters away and uses different
vocabulary entirely. What 2.062 forecloses is narrower and more exact: a
purely logical route from "this atomic fact obtains" to "so does (or
does not) that one," with nothing but the two atomic facts themselves to
carry the inference.

## Zen

Al-Ghazali's *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (The Incoherence of the Philosophers,
17th discussion) gives the classic occasionalist argument against
necessary connection: when fire touches cotton and the cotton burns,
philosophy after Aristotle says the fire *causes* the burning, but
al-Ghazali asks what in reason forces this. Observation shows only that
burning follows contact, habitually — *iqtiran*, mere conjunction, not
a necessity reason can certify. From the cotton's contact with fire
alone, nothing can be strictly inferred about its burning; only God's
uniform habit (*'ada*) supplies the connection, and God could, without
contradiction, create the touching without the burning. Two distinct
existents, and no inference from one to the other on the strength of
reason alone: the shape is close to 2.062's own claim that no Sachverhalt's
existence lets you infer another's.

The disanalogy is where each tradition spends the gap its argument opens.
Al-Ghazali's occasionalism does not leave the gap empty; it fills it with
a maximally strong positive claim — a single, ceaselessly active divine
will re-creating the habitual pairing at every instant, so that what
looked like natural necessity was real, guaranteed regularity all along,
just relocated from the things to their creator. Wittgenstein's 2.062
supplies no such filler and is not looking for one. The absence of a
licensed inference between atomic facts is not a puzzle 2.062 sets out
to solve with some other guarantor standing behind the scenes; it is
simply where the sentence stops. One tradition finds the missing
necessity and hands it to God; the other states the missing necessity
and leaves the gap exactly as wide as it found it.
