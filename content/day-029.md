## Explanation

2.0231 drew the line: substance can fix a form, never a material
property; material properties are only ever made, downstream, once
objects are combined. "Beiläufig gesprochen: Die Gegenstände sind
farblos." Roughly speaking: objects are colourless. This is that line
taken to its blunt, single-word illustration — and the hedge that opens
the sentence is doing as much work as the claim itself.

*Beiläufig gesprochen* — roughly speaking, said in passing, by the way —
is not modesty about how confident the claim is. It is a warning about
how literally to take it. Read flat, "objects are colourless" sounds
like a report on a missing paint job: as if objects were the kind of
thing a colour could have attached to, or failed to attach to, the way
a wall can be painted or left bare. But 2.0231 has already ruled that
picture out. Colour is a material property, and material properties
belong to configurations, not to any object taken alone; asking whether
a bare object *has* colour, or *lacks* it, is already the misdirected
question 2.0231 warned against. "Colourless" is not a fact discovered
about objects. It is colour — the easiest material property to picture,
the one closest to hand — borrowed as an example of the whole excluded
class, then immediately hedged, because even saying it that way still
sounds like ascribing a property, absence though it is.

This is why 2.0232 earns no number of its own beyond a decimal
extension of 2.023: it adds no new claim, only a picture for one already
made. Form is what an object brings to any combination, fixed in
advance, coined at 2.0141. Material properties are what shows up only
after combination, per 2.0231. "Colourless" hands the reader something
to see rather than only something to follow — Wittgenstein's own method
at 4.121, show rather than say, arriving four thesis-numbers early, in
service of a claim that isn't really about paint at all.

The hedge is also where the book's two great readings quietly pull
apart, without needing to argue about it yet. Read traditionally, "roughly
speaking" is ordinary philosophical caution — a placeholder image inside
a metaphysical account still under construction, not to be leaned on too
hard. Read resolutely, the same hedge is the text catching itself in the
act: an early, local tremor of the same self-undermining that later
becomes explicit at 6.54, when the whole apparatus of object-talk is
declared nonsense to be climbed through and discarded. Nothing here
forces a choice between the two. The sentence is compatible with both,
and stays that way.

## Zen

Locke's primary/secondary quality distinction (*An Essay Concerning
Human Understanding*, II.viii) draws a structurally close line: colour
is the textbook example of a secondary quality, a power an object has
to produce a certain sensation in a perceiver, not a resemblance of
anything sitting in the object itself. Pull the perceiver out of the
picture, on Locke's account, and colour goes with them — what remains
in the object are only primary qualities: bulk, figure, texture,
motion. Objects, stripped of an observer, are colourless in Locke's
sense too.

The disanalogy is where the two accounts stop agreeing, and it is not
decorative. For Locke, colour still belongs to the object, even absent:
it is grounded in the object's own primary qualities, a disposition its
actual corpuscular structure carries whether or not anyone is there to
be affected by it. The object keeps hold of colour as its own latent
power. 2.0231–2.0232 will not allow that anchor. Material properties are
not powers objects carry in reserve; they are *erst* — first, only —
formed by the configuration of objects with other objects, a fact about
combination, not a disposition stored inside any single object waiting
for a perceiver to trigger it. Locke detaches colour from the object's
surface but keeps it tethered to the object's constitution. Wittgenstein
cuts the tether entirely: colourlessness here is not absence of a
hidden power, it is absence of anywhere for such a power to be stored in
the first place.
