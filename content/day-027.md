## Explanation

2.022 asked what any imagined world, however unlike the real one, must
still share with it — some "something," named there only as *Form* — and
left the something itself unsaid, promised for tomorrow. Tomorrow is
today. "Diese feste Form besteht eben aus den Gegenständen." This fixed
form consists of the objects. The *eben* is doing real work and is easy
to lose in translation: not "as it happens" but closer to "precisely,"
"just this and nothing more" — the word of a debt being paid exactly,
not a new discovery being sprung. 2.022 said a form must be shared;
2.023 says what it is made of, and treats that as settling the question
rather than opening one.

Nothing named here is new material. *Form* was coined at 2.0141, for a
single object: its fixed range of possible occurrence in atomic facts.
2.022 scaled the same word up to whole worlds without saying what
carried it there. 2.023 closes that gap by identity, not by adding a
third thing standing between object-level form and world-level form:
the world's shared form just is the objects themselves, the same ones
2.021 already named as substance. Two sentences that looked like they
were reaching for different things — 2.021's "the objects are the
substance of the world" and 2.022's "some form is shared by every
possible world" — turn out, as of this sentence, to be talking about
one and the same fact from two angles: substance secures that there is
something fixed; form names what fixed means; 2.023 says the something
and the fixed range are the same objects under two descriptions.

That leaves a question the sentence pointedly does not answer: fixed
in *what respect*? An object can be combined this way or that, and
that combinatorial range is what 2.0141 called its form — but that says
nothing yet about the object's own qualities, the ones that seem, on a
first look, to give it whatever character it has. 2.023 states the
identity and stops there; what a fixed *combinatorial* form does and
does not settle about an object's material character is the next
sentence's business, not this one's.

## Zen

Democritus and Leucippus held that everything real reduces to atoms
and void: fixed, unchangeable, indivisible bodies that never combine
into anything genuinely new, only rearrange, and whose rearrangements
are the whole of change (Aristotle's report, *Metaphysics* A4,
985b4–20). Every world of appearances, however different from another,
is built from the same stock of atoms in different configurations — a
structural match to 2.023's claim that one fixed form, made of the
same objects, underlies every combination a world could take.

The disanalogy is exactly where Democritus keeps going and 2.023 stops.
His atoms are differentiated by *shape, arrangement, and position*
(*rhythmos*, *diathigē*, *tropē*) — physical, spatial properties
intrinsic to the atom itself, the very thing that lets one atom's
bulk and contour cause the tastes and colours of the compounds it
enters (fr. 68 B9, via Sextus Empiricus). Wittgenstein's objects carry
no such freight. 2.023 fixes only that there is a shared form built
from objects; it says nothing about what, if anything, belongs to an
object besides its place in that form — and the sentence immediately
after this one exists to cut off exactly the move Democritus makes,
by denying material properties to the object outright. Democritus's
atoms are the last physical things standing; Wittgenstein's objects,
this thesis alone, do not yet say whether they are physical at all.
