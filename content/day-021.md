## Explanation

Every sentence since 2.01 has added a predicate to a term one day old:
2.011 said combinability is essential to an object; 2.012 said none of
that combinability is accidental, all of it prejudged; 2.013 pictured a
fixed space of possible occurrence; 2.014 extended that space upward into
states of affairs; 2.0141 named the whole thing *form*. 2.02 changes
register rather than adding another floor to the same building: "Der
Gegenstand ist einfach." Four words — the object is simple. Not simple in
the loose sense, easy to grasp; simple in the strict sense analysis
eventually runs out in — without parts, past the point where anything
could be split off and named separately.

Checked against every other thesis in the book, 2.02 is tied for the
shortest sentence in the whole Tractatus — four words in the German,
matched only by 6.4, "Alle Sätze sind gleichwertig," some six hundred
theses later and a different world entirely: an ethical remark near the
top of the ladder, not a claim about objects. The brevity here is not
incidental. Everything the last eight sentences established about an
object's *relations* — what it can combine with, how fixed that space is
— took paragraphs. What an object *is*, in itself, needs four words,
because there is nothing further inside it to describe. A claim of
simplicity is one of the few claims a sentence can make about its target
and, in making it correctly, demonstrate: the sentence asserting
no-further-parts has, itself, no further parts to spare.

2.02 does not argue for this yet — it asserts it, flatly, the way 2.01
flatly asserted that atomic facts are objects combined. The argument,
when it comes, arrives at 2.021 through 2.0212: objects "form the
substance of the world," and if they didn't — if analysis could go on
splitting forever, no fixed bottom anywhere — then whether a sentence had
sense at all would depend on some other proposition happening to be
true, an infinite regress with nothing at the bottom to stop it. That is
still two sentences away. Today only states the conclusion the argument
will retroactively justify: whatever objects are, there is a point where
asking "what are its parts?" has no answer, because it has none.

One loose end from day 9 is worth closing here rather than carrying
further: three predicates were flagged as about to land on "object" in
quick succession — simple (2.02, today), substance (2.021), unalterable
and subsistent (2.027) — with the glossary question deferred until they
had. Only the first has landed. The prose above needed no repeated
definition to carry it — "simple" is close enough to its ordinary sense,
glossed in one clause, that a glossary entry would state what the
paragraph already shows. The real trigger point is still ahead, once
"substance" and "unalterable" have both landed too.

## Zen

Day 10 named this occasion by number and left it there: 2.011 gave
objects *some* essential nature, close enough to the Buddhist concept of
*svabhāva* — own-being — to draw the comparison, but "the full svabhāva
picture — simple, unchanging, substance itself — doesn't arrive until
2.02 and 2.021. The sharper version of this contrast belongs to those
days, not this one." Today is that day for the first of the two.

Simplicity is the strongest form svabhāva can take: not merely a nature
fixed prior to relation, but a nature with nothing under it to be prior
to — no parts a further analysis could turn up. Buddhist philosophy did
not only assert against this in general terms (Madhyamaka's *śūnyatā*,
already used on day 10); a specific school ran a direct argument against
the coherence of a literal partless atom, the paramāṇu of rival realist
schools like Vaiśeṣika. Vasubandhu's *Viṃśatikā* ("Twenty Verses") argues
that a paramāṇu surrounded on all sides by six neighbouring atoms — one
from each direction — must present six distinguishable faces, one toward
each neighbour, or else all seven atoms would collapse onto a single
point and every composite thing would remain exactly atom-sized no
matter how many atoms it gathered. But a thing with six distinguishable
faces has parts, one per face — so the very atom posited as partless
turns out, on the only description available of how it could occupy
space among others, to have parts after all. No coherent partless unit
survives the argument; what looked like the bottom of analysis dissolves
under the first question asked of it.

The disanalogy is exact enough to state plainly: Vasubandhu's target is a
*spatial* atom, something that must have a position among neighbours and
therefore faces turned toward them. 2.02 commits to no such thing. 2.0131,
five days ago, already showed that not every object is spatial — a
pitch, a shade of colour, a degree of hardness are objects too, each with
its own kind of possibility-space, none of them arranged with six sides
facing outward. Wittgenstein's simplicity is a logical requirement on
what a name can mean, not a physical claim about extension in a void; the
six-sided regress has no purchase on something that was never claimed to
have sides. Worth flagging without unpacking: the *argument* Wittgenstein
will give for needing simples at all — a regress that threatens sense
itself if there is no fixed bottom, arriving at 2.021–2.0212 — has almost
the same shape as Vasubandhu's regress, run toward the opposite
conclusion. One tradition finds that the regress can be stopped, and
must be, on pain of collapsing all sense. The other finds that it cannot
be, and takes that to be the discovery rather than the danger.
