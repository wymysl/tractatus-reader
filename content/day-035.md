## Explanation

Every thesis since 2.023 has stayed at the scale of a single object: form
as identity with the objects themselves (2.023), form paired with content
(2.025), form itemized into space, time, and colour (2.0251) — three
sentences running, all about what an object brings to combination. 2.026
changes scale without warning. "Nur wenn es Gegenstände gibt, kann es
eine feste Form der Welt geben." Only if there are objects can there be a
fixed form of the world. Not a form *of* an object any longer — a form of
the *world*.

The grammar is worth reading exactly, because it claims less than it
might look like it claims. "Nur wenn... kann" is a necessary condition,
not a sufficient one: objects existing is what a fixed world-form
requires, not what guarantees it. 2.0231 used the same restrictive
shape three days ago — substance *can only* fix a form, never a material
property — and the discipline is the same here: state exactly how much
follows, no more. What follows is this much: take objects away, and
there is nothing left for "a fixed form of the world" to mean. Whatever
that phrase names, it stands or falls with there being objects to have
it.

That phrase itself has been on this project's books for thirty days.
1.13 named "logical space" — the facts, located somewhere, are the
world — and promised that what the space actually is would be chapter
2's business, running to the end of the book. 2.013 paid the first
instalment: a single thing's own space of possible atomic facts, fixed
the moment the thing is given. 2.026 is where that promise scales up
from one thing's space to the world's: the same fixity 2.013 found in
a thing's possibilities, running through every object there is, is what
a *world's* fixed form would have to be built from. Nothing new is
discovered about what grounds it — 2.021 already named objects the
world's substance, the floor analysis reaches and cannot get under.
2.026 draws out what that floor secures at the scale 1.13 first raised
and left standing open: not just that each thing has a settled space of
possibilities, but that the whole world does, prior to and independent
of which possibilities happen to be actual.

2.027, next, will say why the dependency in 2.026 holds and not just
that it does: "the fixed, the existent, and the object are one." Today
states the conditional; tomorrow collapses it into an identity.

## Zen

Parmenides' *On Nature* argues that only what-is can be thought or
spoken of at all, and that what-is must therefore be ungenerated,
imperishable, whole, and unmoving — the fixed ground beneath any
account of a cosmos, against the flux the poem's mortals mistake for
knowledge (DK B2, B8). The shape of the argument matches 2.026 closely:
a stable, orderly world is possible only *because* something fixed
underlies it; take the fixed ground away and no coherent cosmos, only
Heraclitean drift, remains available even to describe.

The disanalogy sits exactly where the fixity is located. Parmenides buys
his fixed ground at the price of plurality itself: what-is is one,
without parts, without differentiation, because any distinction within
it would be a passage from is to is-not, which the poem rules out from
the start. Change and multiplicity are not merely absent from the
fixed ground — they are what the argument exists to unmask as illusion.
2.026's fixity runs the other way. What is fixed is not a single
undifferentiated One but many distinct objects (2.02's simples, 2.0233's
things that can differ without differing in any nameable property), and
their fixity is exactly what makes a plural, changing world possible
rather than what excludes one: 2.0271, next but one, will call the
object "the fixed, the existent" and the *configuration* — many objects,
combined one way rather than another — "the changing, the variable."
Parmenides needs stasis all the way down to get a cosmos at all.
Wittgenstein needs fixity only at the bottom, in the objects, precisely
so that everything built on top of them can vary.
