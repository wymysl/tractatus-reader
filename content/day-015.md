## Explanation

2.0123 said that to know an object is to know all the possibilities of
its occurrence in atomic facts, and that this is not overreaching because
those possibilities lie in the object's nature. 2.01231 now names the
distinction that claim was already leaning on, and makes it explicit:
knowing an object is knowing "not its external but all its internal
qualities."

The distinction is worth taking slowly, because "know everything about
an object" sounds, on first hearing, like an impossible demand — a
lifetime could not exhaust every fact true of even one thing. 2.01231
narrows the target sharply. External qualities are whatever an object
happens to have without having to have it: where it currently sits, what
color it was painted, who owns it, how it came to be here. None of these
touch what the object is; change any of them and the object survives,
still itself. Internal qualities are the opposite kind: whatever the
object cannot lack and remain that object at all. Full knowledge, in
2.0123's sense, was never asking for the external list — the one that
really could run forever, and that no one has ever finished for
anything. It asks for the internal one, which is a different kind of
thing entirely: not longer or shorter, but closed. There is a last
internal quality to know, the way there is a last legal move for a
chess piece to have, even though there is no last external fact about
where the piece has stood.

This is also why "all" was the right word for 2.0123 to lean on and not
an exaggeration. Once "know an object" is read as 2.01231 specifies it —
internal qualities only — knowing all of them stops sounding like an
infinite task assigned to a finite mind and starts sounding like what
knowing a thing's nature, rather than its history, was always going to
mean. The bishop's legal moves are not a list gathered by watching many
games; they follow at once from what a bishop's move-rule is. 2.01231
says the same holds for objects generally: internal qualities are not
harvested one at a time from experience, the way external facts are —
they are read off the object's nature as soon as that nature is grasped,
which is what makes "all of them" a demand a finite act of knowing can
actually meet.

## Zen

Aristotle's essential/accidental distinction (*Categories*, *Topics*)
draws what looks like the same line: an accidental property (Socrates is
seated, is pale, is in the market) can be gained or lost without
Socrates ceasing to be Socrates; an essential property (rational, or —
in the technical sense — animal) cannot be lost without the thing
itself ceasing to be what it is. Both distinctions sort a thing's
qualities into a kind that could be otherwise and a kind that could not,
and both make knowledge of the second kind, not the first, the target of
real knowing rather than mere acquaintance.

The disanalogy is where the two systems actually stop matching, and it
is a disanalogy about method, not just result. Aristotle reaches
essence through genus and differentia — Socrates is essentially what he
is because he falls under "animal," further narrowed by "rational," a
structure built by comparing Socrates to other things and sorting kinds
within kinds. None of that apparatus is available to a Tractarian
object. Objects, this book has already been building toward and will
say outright soon, are simple — indefinable, not composed of a genus
narrowed by a differentia, nameable but not describable by breaking them
into parts. An object's internal qualities are not arrived at by
placing it in a hierarchy of kinds; the whole point of 2.01231, read
against 2.0122, is that they are already exhausted by what the object's
own possibilities of combination are, with no wider classification
doing any of the work. Aristotle needs a world of many things, compared,
to say what any one of them essentially is. Wittgenstein's object needs
nothing outside itself for its internal qualities to be exactly what
they are.
