## Explanation

Two moves in one sentence, the second boxed in parentheses. The main
clause names something new: not existence alone, and not non-existence
alone, but both together — "the existence and non-existence of atomic
facts is the reality." *Wirklichkeit*, reality, is the word chapter 1
never needed for the pairing 2.05 built yesterday. Then the parenthesis
names the two halves of that pairing separately: an atomic fact's
existence, we also call a *positive fact*; its non-existence, a
*negative fact*.

The parenthesis is the quieter move, and the one worth slowing down for.
Thesis 2 said: "what is the case, the fact, is the existence of atomic
facts." Read that against today's parenthesis and it says something 2
itself never had occasion to say — thesis 2's plain, unqualified "fact"
was, all along, what today finally calls a *positive* fact. It only
needed the qualifier once it had a sibling to be told apart from.
Nothing about what a fact is has changed since day 8; what changed is
that its opposite number — the non-existence of an atomic fact, sitting
unnamed in 2.05 as mere "what does not exist" — now gets a name of
exactly the same shape. Two symmetric nouns for two symmetric cases,
where before there was one noun and a gap.

Reality, so defined, is not yet the same size as "world." 2.04 built
*Welt* from only one of these two ingredients — the totality of
*existent* atomic facts, the positive side alone. 2.06's *Wirklichkeit*
is built from both sides at once, existence and non-existence together.
As things stand after today, reality is the broader-shaped word: world
names one half of what reality names. A few sentences ahead, 2.063 will
assert "the total reality is the world" — collapsing that difference
back down, total reality onto world after all. Whatever argument
licenses that collapse belongs to 2.063, not to today; 2.06 only opens
the gap 2.063 will need to close, and states it fully symmetrically,
with no hint yet of which side is doing the work.

Worth flagging, without settling, where the traditional and resolute
readings part ways on the parenthesis specifically. "Negative fact" is
not innocent vocabulary — it names one side of an actual historical
dispute. Russell, in the 1918 lectures on logical atomism that grew out
of his conversations with Wittgenstein, defends negative facts as a
real, if uncomfortable, addition to the world's furniture: something
has to make "the atomic fact does not obtain" true, and for Russell that
something is a fact, just a negative one. Others in the same debate
(Raphael Demos, later Ramsey) balked at the idea that absence needs its
own positive-sounding item to underwrite it. The traditional reading
inherits this as a live metaphysical question: are negative facts really
there, alongside positive ones, or is "negative fact" a grammatical
convenience mistaken for an inventory item? The resolute reading declines
the question at a different level — on that view neither positive nor
negative facts are stateable furniture of the world at all; the whole
apparatus, symmetric parenthesis included, is scaffolding the reader is
meant to climb past by 6.54, not a completed catalogue to argue over the
size of. Nothing here decides between them; today's sentence reads the
same either way.

## Zen

"Omnis determinatio est negatio" — every determination is a negation —
the formula Hegel drew from a remark in Spinoza's correspondence and
made famous: to say fully and positively what a thing is, is already to
mark off everything it is not. The resonance with 2.06 is real: both
put a positive assertion and a negation in the same breath, as two
faces of one act rather than two unrelated topics.

The disanalogy is exact, and it is the whole difference. For Spinoza and
Hegel, the negation is *inside* the determination — to determine this
desk positively already excludes, in that very determining, its being a
chair, a cloud, a number. One act, two faces, inseparable. 2.06's
negative fact is not that. A positive fact (this atomic fact obtains)
and a negative fact (some other atomic fact does not) are two entirely
separate items, each already complete on its own terms — 1.21 said
atomic facts are switched independently, and nothing in 2.06 revokes
that. Reality needs both named and set side by side precisely because
neither one contains or implies the other; Hegel's formula needs only
one act, because for him the negating was never a second thing to add.
Two traditions reaching for the same pair of words, one finding them
fused, the other finding them merely adjacent.
