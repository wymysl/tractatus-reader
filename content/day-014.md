## Explanation

2.0121 said an object apart from the possibility of its connections is
unthinkable; 2.0122 said that possibility, taken whole, is what the
object's independence *consists in*. 2.0123 turns both into a claim
about knowledge: "if I know an object, then I also know all the
possibilities of its occurrence in atomic facts."

Read "all" as doing the actual work of the sentence, not as ordinary
emphasis. To know an object even a little, in the ordinary sense — to
have met it, handled it, used it in a few sentences — is not what
2.0123 means by knowing it. Knowing the object, full stop, in the sense
this book means, already includes every combination it could ever enter,
not most of them, not the common ones, not the ones encountered so far.
There is no partial version of this knowledge that 2.0123 is describing;
either you know the object, in which case you already have the whole
space of its possibilities, or you don't yet know it at all.

The parenthetical explains why this is not an absurd demand: "every such
possibility must lie in the nature of the object." This is 2.0122 again,
put to work rather than restated for its own sake — the possibilities
are not a list gathered from outside, one discovery at a time, the way
you might learn a stranger's habits by watching them for years. They are
internal to what the object is, the way legal moves are internal to what
a bishop is. Knowing the object *is* knowing its nature, and its nature
already contains the whole space. Nothing here adds a second claim to
the day; it is the reason the first sentence is not overreaching.

The last sentence draws the consequence that makes 2.0123 more than a
restatement: "a new possibility cannot subsequently be found." If
knowledge of an object really were partial — built up empirically,
fact by fact — then in principle experience could always add one more:
a combination nobody had tried yet, waiting to be found later. 2.0123
rules that out categorically, not because objects are rare and well
studied, but because there is nothing there *to* find later. The
possibilities were never hidden in the world, waiting on the far side of
more looking; they were already whichever ones the object's nature
fixed, the moment the object was that object. What full knowledge closes
off is not merely ignorance — it is the very possibility of future
surprise. 2.0124 takes the next step outward: what happens to the space
of atomic facts once every object, not just one, is known this
completely.

## Zen

Day 11 flagged a candidate for exactly this occasion without knowing yet
which thesis would call for it: Spinoza's *scientia intuitiva*, the
third and highest kind of knowledge in the *Ethics*, which grasps a
thing *sub specie aeternitatis* — under the aspect of eternity — by
deriving its properties from its essence rather than assembling them
from encounters over time. To know a thing this way, for Spinoza, is to
already possess everything true of it that follows from what it is;
nothing remains for experience to add, because what experience could add
was never separate from the essence in the first place. That is
2.0123's shape exactly: full knowledge as knowledge already-complete,
not knowledge-so-far.

The disanalogy is precise enough to be worth the space it takes. Spinoza's
scientia intuitiva is an achievement — the rare, hard-won endpoint of the
*Ethics*'s whole method, something a mind moves toward across four books
and mostly falls short of; between it and ordinary experience sits a
second kind of knowledge (*ratio*, reasoning from common notions) doing
most of the actual work most of the time. 2.0123 has no such ladder.
"If I know an object" is not a report of a rare philosophical
achievement; it states what knowing an object *means* for any object
whatever, the moment the word "know" is used correctly at all. Spinoza
describes an ideal few minds reach; Wittgenstein describes what the
concept of object-knowledge already requires, achieved or not. Both deny
that later experience can add to genuine knowledge of a thing's nature —
but one is naming a peak, the other a floor.
