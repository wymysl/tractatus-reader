## Explanation

2.0231–2.0232 said what objects cannot supply on their own: material
properties, colour among them. 2.0233 asks a different question about
the same bare objects — not what they *have*, but what makes two of them
*two*. "Two objects of the same logical form are — apart from their
external properties — only differentiated from one another in that they
are different."

Take the qualification first: "apart from their external properties."
External properties are the ones an object happens to have by standing
in this or that atomic fact — the accidental, changeable ones (2.0231's
material properties are a species of these). Set those aside, and hold
logical form fixed too, so the two objects are alike in the one respect
2.01–2.0141 said an object has fixed in advance: what it can combine
with. What is left to tell them apart?

Nothing describable. That is the sentence's whole point. Once form is
shared and external properties are bracketed, no further fact about
either object — no feature, no relation, no further predicate — remains
available to single one out from the other. And yet the sentence still
insists there are two, not one: they are different. The word "only" is
doing the same work here that "roughly speaking" did in 2.0232 — marking
the floor beneath which explanation cannot go. Distinctness, for objects
this stripped down, is not grounded in anything further. It is not
inferred from a difference found; it is what remains once every
describable difference has been cleared away and two objects are still,
brutely, two.

This is not yet the sharper case 2.02331 goes on to draw out — what
happens when two things share every property, not just their logical
form, and whether one can still be picked out from the other by
description at all. 2.0233 only sets up the question: it names bare
numerical difference as its own, unborrowed ground, before the next
sentence tests what follows when nothing else is left to lean on.

## Zen

Duns Scotus's *haecceitas* — "thisness" — answers a version of the same
question: two individuals can share a common nature completely (his
example is two angels, or two instances of the same species-form) and
still be genuinely two, not one. What accounts for the difference,
Scotus argues, cannot be any further shared or unshared quality, since
by hypothesis the nature is held in common; it must be a formally
distinct individuating principle added to the nature, this-ness as such,
irreducible to any describable feature. Structurally, this is 2.0233's
own move: distinctness holding independently of any property that could
be named.

The disanalogy is where the two accounts part. Scotus's haecceity is
positive furniture — a "formality," his own technical term for something
short of a full separate thing but more than a mere fact, added to the
common nature to do the individuating work. It is supposed to explain
distinctness, to be what distinctness consists in. 2.0233 explains
nothing further and adds nothing: "they are different" names no
principle, formal or otherwise, that could be pointed to as what the
difference consists in. Where Scotus posits a further entity so that
individuation has a ground, Wittgenstein's sentence stops one step
earlier and refuses the further entity — bare difference is the floor,
not itself grounded in anything, formal or informal. Scotus individuates
with an addition; 2.0233 individuates with a refusal to add.
