## Explanation

"Das Bild stellt die Sachlage im logischen Raume, das Bestehen und
Nichtbestehen von Sachverhalten vor." The picture presents the state of
affairs in logical space, the existence and non-existence of atomic
facts.

2.1 announced that picturing happens at all: we make pictures, of facts,
for ourselves. 2.11 is the very next sentence, and it does the first
piece of the work 2.1 left undone — it says what a picture presents.
Two answers arrive at once, and both are borrowed rather than new. The
*where* is logical space, named back at 1.13: facts, that sentence said,
sit in logical space the way lit windows sit among the unlit ones on an
otherwise dark street, each fact meaning something only against the
whole street of ways things could have gone instead. 2.11 puts a picture
on that same street. The *what* is existence and non-existence taken
together — exactly the pairing 2.06 named *Wirklichkeit*, reality, five
sentences ago. A picture, 2.11 says, presents reality: not the bare fact
that something holds, but the fuller thing 2.06 built, which atomic
facts obtain and which do not, side by side.

That second borrowing is the sentence's real content, and it is easy to
read past. A picture that showed only what obtains, with no bearing one
way or the other on what does not, would not yet be presenting a
determinate situation at all — it would be a list, open at one end,
telling you a window or two is lit without telling you anything about
the street. What makes a picture specific, what gives it something to
be right or wrong *about*, is that it commits both ways at once: this
atomic fact in, that one out. 2.11 does not yet say a picture can be
false — that is 2.21's job, four sentences on. It only says a picture is
built with enough content, positive and negative together, that the
question of truth will later be able to get a grip on it.

Ogden's English is worth pausing on here, because it quietly blurs a
distinction the book has been careful about since 1.1. The German has
two different technical nouns in one sentence: *Sachlage*, the state of
affairs a picture presents, and *Sachverhalt*, the atomic fact whose
existence or non-existence makes up that state of affairs. Ogden
translates *Sachlage* here as "the facts" — but *Sachlage* is not
*Tatsache*, actual fact, and the whole point of the sentence is that
what gets presented need not be actual at all; that is exactly why
2.21 still has work to do. Compare 2.202, ninety sentences ahead, where
the identical German phrase, *mögliche Sachlage im logischen Raume*,
gets the correct English: "possible state of affairs in logical space."
One translator, one book, two renderings of one term, ninety sentences
apart. An English-only reader of 2.11 alone would see "presents the
facts ... the existence and non-existence of atomic facts" and could be
forgiven for hearing "facts" and "atomic facts" as just two grain-sizes
of the same actual thing, when the sentence's whole force is that a
picture presents a *candidate* situation, one that could turn out
either way.

## Zen

The *Yijing*'s sixty-four hexagrams are built the way 2.11 builds a
picture: six line-positions, each cast as broken or unbroken, existence
or non-existence at every position, against a fixed space of every way
the six could go. A cast hexagram is exactly a determinate configuration
inside that whole space, chosen out of it the way a Tractarian picture
picks out one arrangement of atomic facts, existent and non-existent,
inside logical space. The combinatorial gesture is the same gesture.

The disanalogy is what each gesture is then used for. A hexagram is cast
*to be read as true of the present matter* — the querent consults it
because the configuration that comes up is offered, with its attached
judgment text, as the actual shape of the situation asked about;
divination is the practice of treating the presented configuration as
already agreeing with reality. 2.11's picture does no such thing, and
could not yet, on what 2.11 alone says. It presents a configuration and
stops there, entirely silent on whether that configuration is the one
that in fact obtains — silent on purpose, since agreement or
disagreement with reality is a further relation the book does not
introduce until 2.21. The *Yijing* collapses the gap between presenting
and asserting in the single act of the cast; the Tractatus holds that
gap open for four more sentences before it lets a picture claim
anything at all.
