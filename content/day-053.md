## Explanation

"Den Gegenständen entsprechen im Bilde die Elemente des Bildes." To the
objects correspond, in the picture, the elements of the picture. 2.12
named the relation between picture and reality: a model, something built
the same way as what it models rather than something that resembles it.
2.13 is the first sentence to say what that building consists in, down
at the level of parts. Not "the picture has elements" — that would be
trivial, true of any arrangement of marks. The claim is a pairing: each
object in the *Sachlage* the picture presents has, somewhere in the
picture, an element that is its counterpart. Toy car to real car, in
2.12's courtroom model; later, at 3.202 and after, a simple sign in a
proposition to the simple object it means.

Two things this sentence does not yet say, on purpose. It does not say
*how* an element manages to correspond to its object — what act or
convention sets up the pairing. That is 2.131's word next, *vertreten*,
"stand for" or "go proxy for," the technical term the rest of the book
will build on. And it does not say what makes a mere collection of
paired elements into a picture of a *state of affairs* rather than a
list — that needs the elements combined "in a definite way," 2.14's
addition. 2.13 isolates the first piece cleanly: a picture has as many
significant parts as there are objects it is about, and the correspondence
runs one-to-one. Get this wrong — leave an object out, or use one element
for two objects — and there is no longer a model of *this* *Sachlage*, of
this configuration and no other; there is a different, or no, model.

The word *Gegenstände*, objects, is worth pausing on: not "things in
general" but the objects of 2.01's *Sachverhalt*, the plain, simple items
whose configuration a state of affairs is. 2.13 is the exact place the
picture theory's parts are pinned to the ontology's parts, sentence by
sentence rather than in one sweep — chapter 2 built the world out of
objects in states of affairs; now it builds pictures out of elements
standing over those same objects, one for one.

## Elsewhere

The Ten Ox-Herding Pictures (十牛図), the twelfth-century Chan/Zen sequence
attributed to Kuoan Shiyuan, is a genuine instance of 2.13's exact claim,
not a loose echo of "pictures." Each of the ten images has elements that
correspond to something outside the image: the ox to the seeker's own
buddha-nature, the herder to the practitioner, the rope and whip to
discipline, the empty landscape of the first image to a nature not yet
recognized. A student is trained to read the correspondence element by
element, the way 2.13 asks of any picture, and the sequence works only
because each element is pinned to its referent and not to some other.

The disanalogy is exact enough to be worth stating rather than assumed.
Wittgenstein's picture needs its correspondence to hold, full stop, for
the picture to be a picture of anything at all; nothing in 2.13 aims past
correspondence. The Ox-Herding series uses correspondence only to retire
it: by the eighth image the ox and the herder are both gone, a plain
circle, empty of elements and so of anything to correspond to anything.
The ninth and tenth images do not restore a better picture — they show
the seeker returning to the marketplace, "gourd in hand," to a world no
longer read through the picture's mapping at all. Chan's series is built
to be climbed past; 2.13's correspondence is what the rest of the book's
picture theory is going to build on, not a device staged for its own
dissolution. A structural cousin of 6.54's ladder, but arriving from a
different direction and, notably, five and a half books earlier.

## Method

The temptation today was to fold 2.131's *vertreten* into this entry
since the two sentences are one thought split in two — "elements
correspond to objects" and "elements stand for objects" say almost the
same thing from opposite ends of the sentence. Checked METHOD's "one day,
one thought" against what each half is actually doing: 2.13 asserts a
pairing exists and is one-to-one; 2.131 names the specific relation
(*vertreten*, "stand for," the verb that reappears at 3.22 for names and
objects) that will carry weight for the rest of the book's semantics.
Kept them separate. The naming move is substantial enough to earn its own
day rather than ride in as a footnote to today's pairing claim, and
collapsing them would have made tomorrow's entry thin rather than making
today's fuller.

The Ox-Herding note needed a check against Kakuan's actual sequence
before writing the disanalogy paragraph, rather than trusting a
half-remembered "ox equals enlightenment" gloss: the crucial move for
today's purpose is specifically the emptying at image eight and the
return in nine and ten, not merely that the pictures are allegorical. Read
against that, the Elsewhere note survives being doubted: it is not "both
traditions use pictures" but a precise claim about what 2.13's
element-by-element pairing needs (permanence) against what the Chan
sequence needs (its own retirement).

No interpretive-war paragraph. Doctrine and resolute readings do not fork
on what 2.13 says; both read it as a straightforward mechanical claim
about picture-elements and objects, with the real interpretive stakes
still ahead at how "stand for" (2.131) and "Name" (3.2ff.) get read. No
METHOD.md change: today's demands — holding one thought to one day when
two sentences are close kin, sourcing an Elsewhere analogy against the
primary sequence rather than a memory of it — are already covered.
