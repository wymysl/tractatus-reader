## Explanation

Yesterday's thesis said what a fact is: the existence of atomic facts.
Today says what an atomic fact is: "a combination of objects." Between
them they explain the word chapter 1 had been using since its very first
line, in two steps rather than one — first down to atomic facts, now
down to what atomic facts are built from.

"Object" (*Gegenstand*) is the new word here, and Wittgenstein glosses it
himself in the same breath — "(Sachen, Dingen)," entities, things. He is
not smuggling in some exotic technical posit; he means, roughly, what
"thing" already means in ordinary talk. What is new is not the word but
its place in the system. 1.1 already ruled out things as what the *world*
is made of — the world is facts, not things. 2.01 does not reverse that.
It says where things belong instead: not directly under the world, but
one level further down, as what an atomic fact is *made of*. Facts are
built from atomic facts (2); atomic facts, in turn, are built from
objects, combined.

The load-bearing word is "combination," and it resists being read as mere
collection. A handful of objects sitting in the same room is not yet, on
this picture, an atomic fact — a heap is not a combination. Something
more structured is meant: objects fitting together in a particular way,
the way a piece of a definite shape locks into a socket rather than
merely touching it. What exactly makes a combination a combination — what
has to be true of an object for it to be combinable at all — is not this
sentence's job to say. That is 2.01's own unfinished business, and the
next several sentences take it up directly.

This is also where the traditional and resolute readings of the book
part ways most sharply, worth saying before either of the next two
sentences tempts a verdict. The traditional reading (Russell's own
introduction to the book, and much commentary since) takes 2.01 as the
opening move of a real metaphysical theory: there really are simple
objects, and they really are what the world is ultimately made of. The
resolute reading (Diamond, Conant) holds that a sentence like this one
cannot, on the book's own terms, state a truth about reality from
outside it — that by 6.54 the reader is meant to recognize 2.01 itself
as one of the rungs to be climbed and then seen as nonsense, not a
discovery to carry away. Nothing this early settles which reading is
right; today's six words read the same either way.

## Method

Revisiting day 2's glossary tripwire, which day 8 flagged as reaching its
real decision point today: 2.01 adds "object," a third technical term
now active alongside fact and atomic fact. Checked directly rather than
deferring again — the explanation above needed exactly one backward
glance ("1.1 already ruled out...") to place object relative to fact, not
a labored re-explanation, and the terms still separate cleanly by what
each one modifies (world/fact, fact/atomic fact, atomic fact/object). No
glossary yet. But the real pressure is close: 2.02 claims objects are
*simple*, 2.021 that they are the world's *substance*, 2.027 that they
are unalterable and subsistent — three more predicates piling onto a
term barely one sentence old. If prose starts doubling back once those
land, that is the day to build it, not before.

Checked 2.01 against the doubt test for a Zen note and found nothing
honest to say yet. The available move — objects as fixed, unanalyzable
simples against Buddhist no-self, or against the momentary dharmas used
on day 8 — is real, but it belongs to 2.02 and 2.027, where the text
actually claims objects are simple, unchanging, the substance of the
world. 2.01 alone only says objects get combined; it has not yet claimed
anything about their own nature for a Buddhist contrast to bite on.
Reaching for the comparison now would spoil those later sentences and
force a note ahead of what today's thesis supports — omitted, per
METHOD's silence-over-filler rule, not for lack of a candidate.

This is also the first day to invoke METHOD's rule on the interpretive
war proper (traditional vs. resolute readings), rather than a different
historical dispute — 1.21's Bradley/Russell disagreement was philosophy
of relations, not this meta-question of how to read the book at all.
Stated both sides in three sentences each and picked no winner, per the
rule; worth keeping it this compressed each time it recurs rather than
letting it grow into a survey of its own.

Also found a genuine, minor wrinkle in the tree view while checking how
2.01 would render. `renderTree()` in `site/app.js` computes depth by
counting the digits after the decimal point, which correctly nests, say,
2.011 one level under 2.01. But Wittgenstein's own numbering pads 2.01
through 2.06 with a leading zero that 2.1 through 2.9 don't carry, even
though both groups are equally first-level comments on proposition 2 —
so the tree will show 2.01 one indent level deeper than its true sibling
2.1 will sit, once 2.1 is eventually reached. Looked for a clean fix
(stripping one leading zero before counting) and rejected it: the same
zero-padding trick recurs at deeper levels too (2.0201 sits one level
under an unlisted "2.020," not under 2.02 directly), so a single
stripping rule that fixes the top case would silently mis-nest cases
like that one. A correct fix needs an explicit parent map, not a smarter
one-line heuristic — real apparatus, disproportionate to a cosmetic
indent on a still mostly-dim branch of the tree. Left as is, with a
one-line comment at the call site recording the limitation. The real
re-check point is whichever day first authors 2.1, when the mismatch
sits visibly beside its own true siblings for the first time.
