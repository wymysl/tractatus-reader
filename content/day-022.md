## Explanation

2.02 stated a property objects have — simple, no parts. 2.0201 is the
first sentence to say what follows from that property for something
other than an object: a *complex*, anything built by combining simples,
and in particular anything a sentence can be about. "Jede Aussage über
Komplexe lässt sich in eine Aussage über deren Bestandteile und in
diejenigen Sätze zerlegen, welche die Komplexe vollständig beschreiben."
Every statement about complexes can be analysed into a statement about
their constituent parts, and into those propositions which completely
describe the complexes. Two pieces, not one: naming the parts is not
enough on its own, because the same parts arranged differently make a
different complex. A sentence about a complex survives analysis only if
it keeps both — which pieces, and how they are put together.

This is the first payoff of 2.02's simplicity claim, arriving one
sentence later rather than three sentences later with the substance
argument (2.021–2.0212). Substance answers *why* there must be a fixed
bottom to analysis at all. 2.0201 answers a different, more practical
question: given that there is one, what happens to an ordinary sentence
— "the watch is on the table," to use an example from nowhere near this
page but the right shape — when you push the analysis all the way down?
It doesn't survive as one sentence. It becomes a set of sentences: which
simples, and a complete specification of their arrangement. The
complex itself never had to be named again once both are given; the name
was shorthand for exactly this, and the shorthand is what analysis
dissolves.

Read forward rather than back, 2.0201 is the seed of a method the book
will spend hundreds of theses making precise — that ordinary language
disguises its real logical form, and that philosophy's job is showing
what a sentence's complete analysis into elementary propositions looks
like (this exact word, *elementary proposition*, doesn't arrive until
4.221, but the operation named there is the one this sentence first
describes). Nothing here yet says such an analysis always terminates, or
how far down "constituent parts" goes before it bottoms out at objects
themselves — 2.02 asserted objects are the floor, but 2.0201 is stated
generally enough to apply one level up, to complexes of complexes, before
it ever reaches that floor.

## Method

Checked, rather than reached for on habit, whether a Zen note belonged
today. The Milindapañha's chariot simile — Nāgasena's argument that
"chariot" names nothing over its parts and their arrangement, that
statements about the chariot resolve into statements about axle, wheel,
frame, and their assembly — is a close, almost exact structural match to
2.0201's claim about complexes: same two-part resolution, parts plus
arrangement, applied to the same kind of target, an everyday composite
object taken as one thing by ordinary language. On strength of parallel
alone this would be one of the closest matches the whole project has
found. Declined it anyway, on diversity-discipline grounds established
days 10–11 and restated as recently as yesterday: day 21 used Buddhist
material (Vasubandhu's paramāṇu regress) one day ago, closing a debt
owed since day 10. Using Buddhist material again immediately, one day
later, reads as the default reach the discipline exists to prevent, not
as an earned second use — even where, as here, the parallel is
genuinely strong rather than coincidental. The discipline is not "use
good parallels," it is "do not let one tradition become the reflex,"
and a two-day gap fails that test regardless of quality. No non-Buddhist
genuine candidate presented itself — mereological reduction of wholes to
parts-plus-arrangement is, as far as I can place it, a much sharper fit
to the Buddhist mereological reductionist tradition specifically than to
anything in, say, Taoist or Vedantic material — so the section is
omitted today rather than filled with a weaker forced comparison from
elsewhere. Worth recording concretely for whichever day next has cause
to reach for Buddhist material: the chariot simile is unused and is the
single strongest candidate on the table, should a thesis arrive that
does not have yesterday's proximity problem.
