## Explanation

2.0123 was about a single object, known completely: every atomic fact it
could occur in, already fixed by its nature, nothing left for later
experience to add. 2.0124 takes exactly the step day 14's explanation
pointed toward — what happens once every object, not just one, is known
this completely. The answer turns on a single word doing all the work:
"thereby" (*damit*). "If all objects are given, then thereby are all
possible atomic facts also given" — not as a further discovery, not as a
second step separately confirmed, but automatically, the moment the
objects are.

Why automatic, rather than a further inference still owed an argument?
Because 2.0123 already put the whole possibility-space inside each
object: to know an object is to know all the atomic facts it could enter.
An atomic fact is a combination of objects (2.01). Each object already
carries its own share of that combining, prejudged in its nature (2.012),
before any combining actually happens. So moving from one object to all
of them adds no further work — there is no separate act of checking
objects against each other still left to do, no assembling of
possibilities the way a catalogue is assembled from separate entries.
The totality of objects and the totality of their possible combinations
are not two facts, one following the other; the second is already
contained in the first, the way 2.0123 already contained it for a single
object.

The italics on "possible" (*möglichen*) are not decoration. 2.0124 fixes
the entire space of what could combine, not which combinations do. It
would be easy to mishear "if all objects are given, all atomic facts are
given" as already announcing the world — as if naming every object told
you what is the case. It does not, and the next few theses make explicit
what 2.0124 already implies: every object sits in a space of possible
atomic facts it could occur in, a space that stays fixed and available
whether or not any given combination in it happens to be actual. Which
of the possible atomic facts *are* actual is a separate matter entirely
— the world's contribution, not logic's — and the book will not reach it
for some theses yet. 2.0124 gives the totality of what could be
arranged. It does not yet say what has been.

## Zen

Leibniz's doctrine of complete concepts has 2.0124's exact shape. Each
individual substance, on Leibniz's account (*Discourse on Metaphysics*
§8; the correspondence with Arnauld), has a concept complete enough to
contain every predicate that could ever truly be said of it — everything
it does, undergoes, or could stand in relation to, already settled in
what the substance is, before any of it happens. Once the totality of
these complete, self-contained concepts is given, the entire realm of
possible worlds is thereby already laid out — not built up world by
world through some separate act of combination, but already implicit
the moment the substances, each internally complete, are given. Totality
of self-contained natures in, totality of possible combinations out,
nothing further to add: 2.0124's move, in a different vocabulary.

The disanalogy cuts at the foundation, not the edges. Leibniz's monads
are windowless: no substance genuinely interacts with or combines with
another; each one unfolds its own predicates from its own concept alone,
and the appearance of one shared world of things combining is guaranteed
from outside, by a pre-established harmony between separately unfolding
substances that never actually touch. A Tractarian object is the
opposite kind of thing all the way down — its whole nature just *is*
its combinability with other objects (2.011, 2.0121); an atomic fact is
objects genuinely combined (2.01), not one substance's private story
running in step with another's. Leibniz needs a harmonizer outside the
system to make many windowless, self-contained natures look like a
single connected world; Wittgenstein's objects need nothing outside
themselves, because their natures already consist in combining directly
with each other.

## Method

Checked a non-Western candidate first before reaching for Leibniz, given
day 15 flagged (without invoking a formal tripwire) that Spinoza and
Aristotle made two non-Buddhist, Western days running. The candidate
considered was the *Yijing*'s generation of the sixty-four hexagrams from
the two elementary line-types: given the elementary units and their rule
of combination, the entire closed space of possible configurations is
thereby fixed in advance of any particular hexagram being cast — a real
structural echo of 2.0124's "thereby." Set it aside because its best
available disanalogy was comparatively thin: the sixty-four-fold closure
is a specific numerological convention (three lines per trigram, six per
hexagram) rather than a difference in the underlying picture, and each
hexagram carries divinatory content a bare space of logical combination
does not. Leibniz's disanalogy — windowless monads needing an external
harmonizer, against objects that combine with each other directly —
cuts into what each system says the whole is built from, not into a
counting convention, which is why it was preferred on the merits rather
than by default. Recording plainly that this makes three Western days
running (Spinoza, Aristotle, Leibniz): the check was real this time, not
skipped, but if a fourth occasion also resolves to a Western candidate
without an equally deliberate look elsewhere, that is the day to treat
the pattern itself as worth a tripwire, the way day 10 eventually did for
Buddhist-family material.

2.0124 is also the clearest occasion yet, since day 9, to ask whether the
possible/actual distinction it leans on needed unpacking beyond the
explanation itself. Decided it does not: 2.013 and 2.0131, immediately
next, are where the book itself develops "space of possible atomic
facts" into an explicit idea, so gesturing at the distinction here and
leaving the fuller unpacking to those theses follows "one day, one
thought" rather than importing their content early.

No pressure on the interpretive-war rule today, for the reason days
